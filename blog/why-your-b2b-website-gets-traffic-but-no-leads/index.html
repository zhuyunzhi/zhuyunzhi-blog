Why Your B2B Website Gets Traffic but No Leads

The seven gates between a visitor and an inquiry — and how to test each one in an afternoon.

A plant manager in Rotterdam searches for the exact product you manufacture. You rank third. The page loads. He stays 47 seconds, scrolls twice, and closes the tab.
You will never know his name. He will never tell you why he left.

Most manufacturers read that as a traffic problem, and the response is always the same: write more articles, post more often, run more ads. But traffic was never the bottleneck. What you had was a decision problem. Somewhere between the search result and your contact form, a buyer was deciding whether you were worth a message — and something in that path told him no.

This article is the audit I run on industrial and B2B websites to find out where that decision goes wrong. It is built around seven gates that sit between a visitor and an inquiry. Each gate comes with a test you can run today, the failure I see most often at that gate, and the smallest change that fixes it.

None of it requires a rebuild. Most of it is copywriting, three screenshots, and one afternoon with your analytics open.

How to use this

You do not need to fix everything. You need to find the gate that is actually leaking.
Run the seven tests in order. The first one that fails is your bottleneck — fix it before you touch anything downstream, because a prettier homepage will not rescue traffic that was never going to buy. If several fail, work outward from the form: the closer a gate is to the inquiry, the cheaper it is to fix and the sooner you see the effect.

Keep a note of what each test tells you. In four weeks you will have something more valuable than a redesign: a list of specific, verified reasons buyers leave.

Gate 1 — Traffic quality: is the right person arriving?

Sessions are not buyers. A site can triple its traffic and produce fewer inquiries, because the new sessions are students, competitors, job seekers, and buyers in countries you do not ship to.

The test. In your analytics, open the landing-page report and look at your top twenty pages by sessions. For each one, ask two questions: how many inquiries did this page produce, and where did those sessions come from?

Three patterns are worth flagging straight away:

  • Pages with traffic and zero inquiries over 90 days. These are usually informational pages — "what is a sandwich panel", "how does cold storage work". Useful for credibility, useless for conversion.
  • A country split that does not match your markets. If most sessions come from places you cannot serve, your headline number is decorative.
  • Sessions from your own country and your own industry. Some of that is competitors; some is your own team. Neither will send a purchase order.

What usually fails. Manufacturers optimize for the keyword they can rank for, not the query a buyer would type. The result is a library of educational content competing for curiosity traffic.

The fix. Separate the two jobs. Keep informational articles for authority and internal linking, but stop judging them on leads. Then build the pages that only a buyer would land on — product pages, application pages, comparison pages, and pages about shipping, certification, and minimum order quantities. That is where inquiries come from.

Gate 2 — The three-second relevance test

A visitor decides in about three seconds whether this is the company he was looking for. Not whether you are good — whether you are relevant.

The test. Open your homepage on a phone and hand it to someone who does not know your company. A colleague from another department, a friend in another industry, ideally someone from your target market. Three seconds. Take the phone back. Ask: what does this company sell, and who is it for?

If they hesitate, the page has failed. If they answer with your industry but not your product, it has half-failed.

What usually fails. The first screen is filled with things that matter to you and nothing that matters to a stranger: the year you were founded, factory area in square metres, a rotating carousel of stock photos, and a slogan like "quality first, customer foremost". Every one of these is a reason to keep scrolling — and most visitors will not.

The fix. Rewrite the first screen so that, top to bottom, it answers four questions in plain language:

  1.  What you make — described in the words of your buyer's problem, not your catalogue's words
  2. Who it is for — the industries or use cases you serve best
  3. Where you are, and whether you export and to which regions
  4. One photograph that shows the product doing a job in the real world

That is the entire brief. No mission statement, no history, no carousel.

Gate 3 — Proof: does anything back up the claim?

Overseas buyers are cautious for good reason. Sending money to an unfamiliar supplier across a border carries real risk, and a buyer who has been burned once will not risk it twice. If your site gives him nothing to verify, he will not ask questions — he will leave quietly.

The test. Imagine you are a buyer in Germany who has never heard of you. Using only your website as a starting point, answer these five:

  • Can I confirm this company legally exists, and does that name match the domain and the bank account?
  • Is there evidence of real production, or only stock imagery?
  • Can I see reference projects — country, industry, year — even if customer names are confidential?
  • Which certifications apply to my market, and are they current?
  • Is there a named person with a face and a role I can address?

What usually fails. Generic certification logos with no certificates behind them. Project galleries with no context. A contact page that leads to a free mailbox with no name attached. And the small detail that kills confidence fastest: a business email address that does not match your domain.

The fix. Build one verification block — a section that exists only to be checked, not to sell. Company legal name, registration number, factory address on a map, certifications with issuing body and validity, three reference projects with country and year, and a photograph of the person who will answer your email. Put it on your homepage and link it from every product page. It does more work than any paragraph of marketing copy.

Gate 4 — Specificity: can a stranger work out whether you fit?

Specifications are not a detail; they are the buying decision. A buyer cannot judge whether you are suitable — or whether you are affordable — without them.

The test. Take your three best-selling products. For each, can a stranger answer these without emailing you?

  • What it is, including the dimensions or performance range
  • What options exist, and which are standard versus custom
  • Minimum order quantity
  • Lead time from confirmed order to loading
  • What drives the price — material grade, volume, finish, packaging
  • Packing, port of loading, and payment terms

What usually fails. A product page with a name, four stock photos, and a "contact us for details" button. Forcing a buyer to email before he can learn the basics does not generate leads; it filters out the ones with options.

The fix. Publish a specification table on every product page, and add three short paragraphs underneath: how to order, how long it takes, and what changes the price. Ranges are fine — "typically 25 to 35 days depending on finish" is infinitely more useful than silence. If you cannot publish prices, publish the drivers of the price.

Gate 5 — The next step: what exactly should he do?

Many sites have exactly one call to action, somewhere at the bottom: Contact us. That presents every visitor with the same commitment — start a sales conversation — whether he is ready for one or not.

The test. List every action a visitor can take on your site, other than leaving. If the list has fewer than three items, you have a commitment problem.

What usually fails. A single form, plus a phone number that only works in your time zone. Nothing for the buyer who is three months early, nothing for the one who wants to compare you with two competitors, and nothing for the engineer who just wants a drawing.

The fix. Build a ladder of steps, from smallest to largest:

  • Download a specification sheet or technical drawing — no form, no gate
  • Request a sample, a quote, or a drawing in your format
  • Talk to a person — a short form or a booking link
  • Verify — the proof block from Gate 3

Place the small steps inside content, and the larger ones at the end of it. A buyer who starts by downloading a drawing is already in your pipeline, and you did not have to ask him for anything.

Gate 6 — Form friction: how much work did you ask for?

The form is where interested visitors become leads — or stop being either.
The test. Open your own contact form on a phone and fill it in, timing yourself. Then check three things: where the message goes, whether a confirmation page exists, and whether your analytics records a submission as a conversion.

What usually fails.

  • Too many required fields. Address, phone, and company size are questions for the second email, not the first.
  • No stated response time. "We will reply within one business day" measurably reduces abandonment.
  • A destination nobody monitors. Contact-form messages routed to a shared inbox frequently become spam, or simply get buried.
  • No thank-you page. Without it, you cannot measure conversions at all — and you will spend the next year guessing.
  • No test submissions. A surprising number of forms break silently after a plugin update or a hosting change.

The fix. Four fields: name, email, country, and what you need. Add one sentence promising a reply window. Send submissions to a specific person and a backup address. Redirect to a thank-you page after submit. Then put a recurring reminder in your calendar to send a test submission once a month — it takes two minutes and it catches everything.

Gate 7 — Response time: the one you control completely

This gate has nothing to do with your website, and it is often the one losing the most business.

The test. Look at your last twenty inquiries and record the time between the message arriving and your first reply. Not the reply with prices — just the first human response.

What usually fails. Replies go out in two to five days. Meanwhile, the buyer has sent the same request to four other suppliers, and the first substantive answer he receives becomes the benchmark everyone else has to beat. In many categories, the deal is effectively decided in the first 24 hours, long before price is compared.

The fix. A first reply within one business day, three sentences long: confirm receipt, state what happens next, and ask the one question that unblocks you. If you can already give a rough price range or a lead time, give it. Speed reads as competence, and it costs you nothing but a habit.

The 30-day order of work

Do not fix these in the order they appear above. Fix them in this order:

Week 1 — Gates 6 and 7. The form and the response habit. Cheapest changes, fastest effect, no design work.
Week 2 — Gates 2 and 5. The first screen and the ladder of next steps. A day of writing, applied to your highest-traffic pages.
Week 3 — Gate 4. Specifications, lead times, and price drivers on the three products that bring the most traffic.
Week 4 — Gate 3. The verification block, then a recurring monthly habit of reviewing Gate 1 in your analytics.

At the end of the month, compare inquiries against your starting number. If traffic is unchanged and inquiries moved, you found the real bottleneck. If nothing moved, you have narrowed the problem to traffic quality — which is a much better conversation to be having than "the website does not work".

What this is not

This is not a case for redesigning your site, changing your platform, or posting more content. Those are answers to questions you have not asked yet.

The uncomfortable part of the audit is that most of the failures it finds are not technical. They are things you already knew but had never written down: what you sell, for whom, at what minimum, in how long, backed by what proof, answered by whom. Buyers are not asking for a better website. They are asking for those answers, in a form they can check.

Write them down, publish them where a buyer can find them, and put a name at the end of the contact form. That is the whole job.

The Follow-Up Email That Revives a Dead Inquiry

An enquiry arrives on Monday. You reply within the hour, fully, with prices. On Wednesday you send a small clarifying question. Nothing. By Friday the thread is cold, and you are looking at a blank email compose window, trying to work out what to say.



Most people write something like just checking in — let me know if you have any questions. It feels polite. It is also, statistically, one of the least effective sentences in export sales, because it does three unhelpful things at once: it adds no information, it hands the entire next step to someone who is busy, and — read from the other side of the world — it sounds like pressure.



A follow-up is not a reminder. It is a small, self-contained reason for the other person to type one line back.



Why most follow-ups fail



Three failure modes account for almost all of them.



It asks without giving. A message that only requests something creates work for the reader. The reader is already behind. Your email joins the list of things that require effort.



It contains nothing new. If your follow-up could have been sent on the day of the original email, the buyer has no reason to read it now. Reactivating a thread means changing something: new information, a new constraint, a new option.



It reads as chasing. "Just following up" and "circling back" carry an unspoken you owe me an answer. Even when that is true, saying it out loud makes the reader's next action feel like an obligation, and obligations get postponed.



The fix is not to write a better chasing email. It is to write something that is not a chase at all.



The three parts of a follow-up that works



1. One specific, cheap question. Not "do you have any questions" — that is an invitation to compose an essay. A question the reader can answer in a line: is the delivery address a port or an inland site? Is the quantity for one site or a programme? Is the finish specified by the client or by you? A cheap question has an obvious answer sitting in their head already.



2. Something new. Anything genuinely new counts — a lead time that moved, the end of a price validity window, a photo of a similar job, a change in a certification or a standard, a component you now hold in stock. The rule is simply: could this paragraph have been written on day one? If yes, it is not new, it is repetition.



3. A default action. State what you are going to do next, and when, unless you hear otherwise. Not as a threat — as a way of letting the reader steer with one word instead of writing a paragraph. "If the destination is a port I will quote on FOB; if it is inland, I will quote to site. I will send the first version on Thursday either way." Now silence has a defined outcome, which makes replying cheap.



Notice what has happened to the cost of answering. Instead of "read my email, remember the project, formulate a response", the reader is being asked to confirm one fact, or to say no, wait. That is the whole technique. Everything else is tone.



Send on events, not on a calendar



The most common advice is to follow up on day three, day seven, day fourteen. That is useful for remembering to do it, and slightly wrong about when it will work.



Follow-up is effective when you have something new, and the calendar does not know when that happens. The coating line got booked out. The shipping rate for that lane moved. A batch of samples came off the line. A standard was updated. Each of those is a real reason to write, and each arrives on its own schedule.



So use the calendar only as a reminder to look: is there anything new I can say? If there is, send it. If there is not, waiting is better than padding.



Three emails you can copy



They are meant to be adapted, not sent as they are. Names, products and specifics are placeholders.



Day 3–5 — one question



Subject: one question on the [product] enquiry Hi [Name], Before I put a full quote together, I want to check one thing: is the delivery destination a port, or an inland site? It changes the packing specification and the freight line, so I would rather get it right the first time than revise it later. A one-line reply is enough — either is fine. [Signature]



Day 10–14 — new information plus a default action



Subject: updated lead time for [product] Hi [Name], Two things have changed since my last email, so I am passing them on rather than asking whether you are still interested. The surface treatment line is now booked until the 20th, which moves a confirmed order from four weeks to five. If your site date is fixed, tell me and I will look at splitting the batch. We also have samples from the last production run in the workshop. I can send two by courier this week if it helps you compare materials. If neither point affects your timeline, say so and I will hold off until you are ready. [Signature]



Day 30 — close it, politely



Subject: closing the file on [product] Hi [Name], I am tidying our enquiry list and this one has been open since March, so I am going to close it and stop sending updates. If the project is still live and the timing was simply wrong, reply with one line and I will reopen it with current pricing. If it has gone elsewhere, no reply is needed. I would rather know it is closed than keep guessing. [Signature]



That last email frequently produces the highest reply rate of the three. It works because it removes the obligation entirely, and because people are more willing to answer a question when the alternative is losing the option.



Things that quietly undo the effort



Sending three follow-ups in a week. The second one arrives before the first has been read on a busy desk. Space them, and space them by events.



"I assume my last email did not reach you." It did. Everyone knows it did. The line reads as an accusation dressed as a courtesy.



Longer messages each time. A follow-up should be shorter than the thing it follows. Length signals anxiety, and anxiety is not a reason to reply.



Attaching the entire quotation again. Give the file only when the message contains a reason to open it.



Writing to a distribution list. If there are three people on the thread and none of them is named as the owner, everyone assumes someone else will answer. Pick one name and put it in the first line.



The one thing to remember



A follow-up is not a nudge. It is a new, small, easy decision offered to someone who has many. You are not trying to make them feel behind — you are trying to make replying cheaper than not replying.



Which means the real work happens before you write the email: find the one question, or the one piece of news, that makes the message worth opening. If you cannot find either, the honest move is to wait — and to spend that time on the quotations that are still being read. Silence after a quote usually is not about you; why it happens, and what to fix in the document instead is a different problem with a different solution.



And if the enquiry never deserved this much attention in the first place, that is worth knowing too — three signals an enquiry is worth your time.

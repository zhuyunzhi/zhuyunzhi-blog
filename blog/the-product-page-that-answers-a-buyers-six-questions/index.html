The Product Page That Answers a Buyer's Six Questions

A product page has one job, and it is not to describe the product.



It is to move a stranger from "I wonder what these people make" to "I have a specific question I can send them". If a page ends with the reader knowing more but having nothing to ask, the page has informed them and failed them at the same time.



Most manufacturing product pages are built like catalogue entries: a photo, a paragraph, a list of materials, and a contact button. That structure answers a question the buyer has not asked yet — what is this — and skips the six they actually have.



Everything that follows is those six questions, in the order a buyer asks them, and what answering each one looks like on the page.



Question 1: What exactly do you make?



Not the category. The specific line.



"Steel structures" is a category. A buyer cannot tell from it whether you fabricate, assemble, supply components, or act as a trading intermediary — and that single ambiguity means the visitor has to email you just to find out whether the conversation is worth starting.



A product page should answer, in the first screen:




  • What the product is, in the buyer's own vocabulary, not your internal one

  • The main specifications — dimensions, materials, capacity, whatever defines it — with units and, where they exist, standard references

  • What varies between variants, so the reader can place their own requirement on your range

  • What it is typically used for, one line, so they can confirm they are in the right place



If a reader has to squint to work out whether you make what they need, they will not scroll to find out.



Question 2: Can you make mine?



The second question is the difference between a supplier and a catalogue.



Very few industrial buyers want the standard item. They want their dimensions, their finish, their packaging, their specification, or their market's compliance requirements. The product page's job is to tell them how far your flexibility extends — and where it stops.



Answer it with three things:




  • What you can customise — dimensions, colour, configuration, branding, packaging

  • What you cannot — the honest boundaries. A page that says "any specification" reads as a page that has not thought about it

  • What customisation does to the other numbers — how it affects minimum quantity, lead time, and price



That last point is the one almost nobody publishes and the one buyers need most. If customising doubles the minimum order, say so. It saves a round of email for both of you, and it signals that you have done this before.



Question 3: Who have you done this for?



Buyers look for a proxy for competence, and the fastest one is who you have already done this for.



This is where factories hesitate, because they assume it means naming clients. It does not. Publication restrictions are normal, and buyers understand them. What works just as well:




  • The type of buyer you serve — contractors, distributors, OEMs, project owners

  • The type of project — residential, industrial, infrastructure, retail

  • The markets you have shipped to, and for how long

  • Scale and repetition — annual volumes, years of supply, repeat customers



An anonymised pattern is still a pattern. "We have supplied distributors in Australia and New Zealand for six years" tells a buyer more than a logo wall with no context, and it does not require anyone's permission.



Question 4: Can you actually deliver it, and when?



Two questions, usually asked together, and usually answered badly.



Capacity. Not the theoretical number. The buyer wants to know whether their order fits into your real production, and whether it fits alongside everything else you are running. Approximate annual capacity, typical order size, and how a new order is scheduled are all answerable without revealing anything sensitive.



Lead time. The single most common failure on manufacturing websites is a lead time with no anchor. "15 days" is not a lead time — it is a number. Fifteen days from order, from deposit, from drawing approval?



Publish the anchor, and publish the variation:




  • Standard lead time, and what it starts from

  • How it changes with quantity or customisation

  • What the realistic range is — a range buyers can plan around beats a single number they cannot



A buyer who cannot plan around your delivery cannot buy from you, regardless of price.



Question 5: How do I know it will be right?



This is the trust section, and it is the one factories most often skip — or fill with adjectives.



"High quality" and "strict quality control" are not evidence. Evidence looks like:




  • Certifications you actually hold, named specifically, with the scope stated. Not a wall of logos — a list of documents, and what each one covers

  • How quality is checked — incoming material inspection, in-process checks, final inspection, and who is responsible

  • Testing capability, whether in-house or through a named third party

  • What happens when something goes wrong — the process for handling a defect, and the fact that such a process exists



The last point is disproportionately persuasive. Every experienced buyer knows defects happen. A supplier who describes how they handle one is demonstrating that they have been through it, which is a stronger signal than any claim of never having any.



Question 6: How do I start?



The final question, and the one that decides whether the page generates an enquiry or a bounce.



A buyer who has read this far has decided the conversation might be worth having. What they need now is a step so specific that there is nothing to think about.



That means:




  • A named contact — a person or a role, not just a generic address

  • What to send — a drawing, a specification, a quantity, a market. Tell them what makes a useful first message

  • What they will get back — a quotation, a feasibility answer, a sample offer, and roughly how long it takes

  • A realistic response time, which you can actually meet



"Contact us for a quotation" is not a next step. "Send your dimensions and destination port, and we will return a quotation in two working days" is. One asks the reader to work out what to do; the other does the work for them.



The page skeleton



Six questions, six blocks, in this order. Nothing here is expensive; it is mostly a matter of writing down what you already know.




  1. Above the fold — what the product is, the defining specifications, and who it is for. One clear line, not a slogan.

  2. Variants and range — the options a buyer can place themselves against.

  3. Customisation — what you can change, what you cannot, and the cost in quantity and time.

  4. Who you serve — buyer types, markets, years, volume. Anonymised is fine.

  5. Capacity and delivery — approximate scale, and lead times with their anchors.

  6. Quality and compliance — documents held, how inspection works, what happens with a problem.

  7. How to start — named contact, what to send, what comes back, how fast.



That is a long page by catalogue standards, and that is the point. A catalogue page is built to be scanned quickly. A product page that converts is built to be read by someone deciding whether to spend an afternoon on you — and the six questions are what that decision is actually made of.

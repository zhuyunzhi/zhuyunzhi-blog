Manufacturer or Trading Company? How to Tell, and Why It Matters

Reading this as a supplier? → Read the other side: Manufacturer or Trading Company — Should You Hide It?



Somewhere in your sourcing process there is a question you have probably asked at least once, usually in an internal email and never to the supplier's face: is this actually a factory?



It is a reasonable question. It is also, often, the wrong one. What you need to know is whether you can run this order with this supplier — and that is not the same as establishing what kind of company they are. A capable trading partner can be a better supplier than a weak factory, and a factory that will not tell you the limits of what it makes is worse than either.



So this is not a guide to catching anyone out. It is a guide to reading what a supplier publishes, and to knowing when the distinction is worth your attention at all.



First: when it does not matter



Start here, because most of the effort people put into this question is wasted.



If you are buying a defined product at a defined specification, and the price, lead time and inspection terms are all checkable, the number of parties between you and the production line changes very little. What you are buying is delivery against a specification. Verify that instead.



If the middle layer is doing real work for you — consolidating several product lines, holding stock, providing local warranty support, extending you credit terms, handling your customs paperwork — then you are not paying a markup for nothing. You are buying a service you would otherwise have to build yourself. That is a legitimate supply arrangement, and its value is measured in what it saves you, not in whether the person owns a welding machine.



If your volumes are small and occasional, and the item is standard, chasing the identity question will cost you more time than it saves. Spend the same afternoon on their delivery record instead.



In all three situations, the honest answer is: it does not matter much, and you should stop worrying about it.



When it does matter



There are four situations where it matters a great deal, and they all share one feature — you are about to depend on something you cannot check afterwards.



You need customisation, tooling, or a specification change. Every technical modification has to be agreed with whoever controls the process. If that person is one step removed, you get a translation layer: your question goes in, an answer comes back, and if the answer is wrong you find out at inspection. This is where a supplier's structure becomes visible whether they want it to or not.



You are going to audit, or you need certifications that attach to a specific site. Audits and site-specific certificates are issued against an address. If the address on the certificate is not the address that will run your order, the document protects someone else's product.



You are planning a long-term, high-dependency relationship. Then capacity, expansion plans and where the critical processes sit are all things you will need to know eventually. Learning them in year three is more expensive than learning them now.



The price contains a lot of technical content. If a good part of what you are paying for is engineering rather than material, you need to understand where that engineering comes from — because that is what you are actually buying, and it is the part that will be hardest to replace.



Six signals you can read from their own materials



Everything below comes from information the supplier has already given you. It requires no investigation, no background checks and no contacting their other customers — and it is enough for a judgement, because you are not trying to prove anything, only to decide how much structure to plan around.



1. Who is named on the certificates. Certificates usually name both the certificate holder and the manufacturing site. On a quality system or product certification, those two names tell you more than any website copy. Check whether the manufacturing address is a site you have seen described anywhere else.



2. How specific the process description is. People who run a process describe it in the vocabulary of that process: the sequence of operations, the equipment, the tolerances, the inspection points, the things that go wrong. Descriptions built from "advanced equipment" and "strict quality control" are marketing language, and marketing language is a signal about who wrote the page, not about capability. It is not proof of anything on its own — many genuine factories write badly — but a technical description is difficult to fake and easy to read.



3. Whether the product range is plausible as one operation. A supplier offering five unrelated categories, each described as their own production, is worth one polite question. It is possible to have several workshops. It is also a common shape for a trading business. Ask how the range is produced and listen to whether the answer is structural or vague.



4. Whether their material is internally consistent. Photos, videos and documents collected over several years should look like the same company: similar buildings, similar equipment, workers in recognisable workwear, packaging done the same way. You are not looking for a contradiction in a single image. You are looking for whether the company has one physical identity or several borrowed ones.



5. How granular the quotation is. Factory quotes tend to break down into process steps: material, fabrication, surface treatment, packing. A single all-in number can indicate a trading layer — though this is a hint, not a test, because a good trader will also quote in detail. Read it alongside everything else.



6. Where the answers go. Ask a narrow technical question — say, how a specific tolerance is measured, or what happens to a component between two process steps. Someone in the operation answers directly; someone in a sales office says they will check. Neither response is dishonest, and you should not treat the second as a failure. But the response tells you what the communication route looks like for the next two years.



You can simply ask, if you ask properly



There is no rule against asking directly. A single clear sentence does it:



Is the manufacturing done at your own facility, or through a partner?



That is a normal commercial question, and a professional supplier will not be offended by it. What you get back is itself the information you wanted. A direct answer with a boundary in it — "these lines are ours, this one is a partner's, which is why the lead time is longer" — is the most useful response you can receive. An answer that restates capabilities without answering the question tells you the structure is being managed.



The one thing to avoid is turning it into an interrogation. Ask once, plainly, and treat the answer as a fact about how they work rather than a verdict on their character.



The conclusion that surprises people



Trading companies are not worse suppliers than factories. Factories are not automatically safer than trading companies. The distinction you were actually reaching for, when you started asking this question, was never who owns the machine. It was how predictable is this supplier — can I estimate the lead time, can I change a specification without losing a week, will the person answering my email be able to act on it.



Judged that way, a trading company that is explicit about what it makes, what it sources, and where the extra two weeks come from is a safer partner than a factory that claims to do everything and answers every technical question by forwarding it. One has told you their shape. The other has told you nothing, and you will discover it during your first claim.



So read the six signals. Ask the direct question once. And then judge the answer against what you actually need from the next order, rather than against a preference for who owns the building.
